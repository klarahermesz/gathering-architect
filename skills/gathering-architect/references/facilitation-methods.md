# Facilitation Methods

Step-by-step instructions for 20+ facilitation methods used in participatory events, workshops, and learning communities. Each method includes purpose, group size, timing, materials, full facilitation sequence, facilitator tips, variations, and source URLs.

## RAPID PARTICIPATION METHODS

Six of these are Klara's own designs. The other two, Think, Pair, Room and The Headline and the Cause, adapt established formats credited in their own Source lines below. All eight are built for getting full-room participation fast: small constraints that stop a few voices from dominating and get everyone contributing. Several other collections work the same territory, and they are worth reading in their own words rather than in ours. The best known is Liberating Structures (Henri Lipmanowicz and Keith McCandless, liberatingstructures.com), whose 33 microstructures are published in full with their own facilitation scripts. Their license is non-commercial only, so check it before running their originals in a paid engagement. Nothing below is drawn from it.

### 1. Think, Pair, Room

**Purpose:** Generate ideas with equal voice for everyone. Stop the loudest few from dominating.

**Group Size:** Unlimited (5-500+)
**Time:** 8-10 minutes per cycle
**Materials:** Paper optional, no other setup needed

**Step-by-Step Facilitation:**

1. **Solo write (90 seconds).** Pose a real question. Everyone writes in silence, no talking, no comparing notes yet.

2. **Pair merge (3 minutes).** Partner up. Compare what you wrote, then agree on ONE idea worth bringing back to the room. The constraint is the point: merging forces sharper thinking than just sharing side by side.

3. **Room share (remaining time).** Each pair states their one merged idea in turn. No repeats. Stop once the themes clearly start recurring.

**Facilitator Tips:**
- Don't let anyone skip the solo write; it's what keeps quieter voices from getting steamrolled in the pair
- If the first cycle felt shallow, run a second one on a sharper question
- Track themes out loud as pairs share, so the room can see its own thinking accumulate

**Variations:** Trios instead of pairs for rooms over 100, written cards instead of a spoken room-share, remote version with breakout rooms for the pair stage

**Source:** Think-Pair-Share, developed by Frank Lyman in 1981. The sequence above is our own adaptation: the one-idea pair merge and the no-repeat rule in the room share are additions to his format. Write-up consulted: https://www.kent.edu/ctl/think-pair-share

### 2. The Second Problem

**Purpose:** Help someone who is stuck. Most stuck people do not need advice, they need the problem restated, because the version they are carrying is usually the wrong one.

**Group Size:** 5 per group
**Time:** 12 minutes per person
**Materials:** Index cards, pens

**Step-by-Step Facilitation:**

1. **Write it down (1 minute).** The person with the problem writes it as a single sentence on a card. They do not say it out loud. Then they pass the card to the person on their left.

2. **Someone else reads it (30 seconds).** That person reads the sentence aloud to the group, exactly as written. Hearing your own problem in another voice is the first useful thing that happens here, and it costs nothing.

3. **Silent reframing (4 minutes).** Everyone except the owner writes alternative statements of the problem, one per card, at least two each. They all start "what if the real problem is." No advice, no solutions, no questions. Just other ways the problem might be described.

4. **Pick the uncomfortable one (2 minutes).** The cards go to the owner. They read all of them and choose the one that makes them most uncomfortable, not the one that seems most accurate. Discomfort is the better signal here, because the accurate-sounding ones are usually the framing they already had.

5. **Advice, but only on the new problem (4 minutes).** Now the group talks. The owner stays in the conversation with one job: interrupt whenever the advice drifts back to the original sentence. They also write down only what they had not already thought of.

Run a second round with a new owner if you have time.

**Facilitator Tips:**
- Guard step 3 hard. The instinct to help arrives fast and it ruins the round. If someone writes a solution, hand the card back
- The owner interrupting is not rudeness, it is the method. Say so before you start, or nobody will do it
- If nobody's reframe lands, that is real information. The problem may be exactly what they said it was, which is worth knowing

**Variations:** Remote version with a shared doc where reframes are typed into a column the owner cannot see until the timer ends. For sensitive topics, collect and shuffle the reframe cards so the owner cannot tell who wrote which

**Source:** Klara's original design.

### 3. Monday 9am

**Purpose:** Convert good intentions into one specific move that has a date on it and someone who will ask about it.

**Group Size:** 6-30, works in a circle
**Time:** 13 minutes
**Materials:** Paper, pens

**Step-by-Step Facilitation:**

1. **Three fast candidates (2 minutes).** Everyone writes three things they could do next week. Speed over quality. Bad ideas are fine at this stage.

2. **Cross out (2 minutes).** Strike any that would take longer than thirty minutes. Strike any you have already told someone you would do, because those have had their chance. Strike any that would leave you with nothing you could show another person. Whatever survives is real.

3. **Write it as a diary entry (3 minutes).** Take one survivor and write it the way it would appear in a calendar: day, start time, how long, and what you will be holding when it is finished. "Tuesday 08:30, 25 minutes, a one-page draft of the invite." Not a goal, an appointment.

4. **Read only the appointment (5 minutes).** Round the circle. Each person reads their calendar entry aloud and nothing else. Fifteen seconds each, no commentary, no reactions, no follow-up questions.

5. **Name your witness (1 minute).** Each person names one other person in the room who will ask them about it on Tuesday. That person answers out loud, yes or no. A no is allowed, and if you get one, ask someone else.

**Facilitator Tips:**
- The "already told someone" cut is the one that does the work. Expect groans, and hold it anyway
- Do not let anyone justify their move during the circle. The whole point of reading only the appointment is that nobody performs
- A witness who says no is doing everyone a favour. Thank them out loud so the next person feels able to

**Variations:** Run it at the close of any longer session to convert discussion into movement. For remote groups, put the appointments in a shared column and pair witnesses in the chat

**Source:** Klara's original design.

### 4. Both/And Statements

**Purpose:** Name the tension a team is actually navigating instead of pretending it's a simple either/or choice.

**Group Size:** 4-6 per group
**Time:** 20 minutes
**Materials:** Paper, markers

**Step-by-Step Facilitation:**

1. **Teach the shape (3 minutes).** "We need to be both ___ and ___, and neither one can lose." Give one example from outside the room.

2. **Draft (6 minutes).** Each group writes 3-4 both/and statements about their actual situation.

3. **Sharpen (6 minutes).** For each statement, ask: "Does this sound generous to both sides, or does it secretly blame one of them?" Rewrite until it does.

4. **Pick one (5 minutes).** Each group names the single statement that's most alive for them right now.

**Facilitator Tips:**
- A good statement makes people a little uncomfortable because it's true, not because it reads as an attack
- Resist the urge to resolve the tension in the room; naming it clearly is the win for this exercise
- Have 2-3 pre-written examples ready if a group gets stuck

**Variations:** Use as a closing exercise to surface what's unresolved before people leave, run individually first for politically sensitive topics

**Source:** Klara's original design.

### 5. The Headline and the Cause

**Purpose:** Stress-test a plan by finding the thing that is already true today and will cause it to fail.

**Group Size:** 5-20
**Time:** 18 minutes
**Materials:** Sticky notes, a wall, markers

**Step-by-Step Facilitation:**

1. **Set the date (1 minute).** Pick a real date, a few weeks after the plan was supposed to have delivered. Say it out loud. Everything from here on is written as though that day has arrived.

2. **Write the announcement (3 minutes).** Silently, everyone writes the internal message that went round on that date explaining that it did not work. One sentence, in the voice a colleague would actually use. Not a newspaper headline, an email subject line.

3. **Post and cluster (4 minutes).** Put them all on the wall. Read them in silence first. Then group them by the failure they describe, not by the words they share. Two or three clusters usually dominate.

4. **Walk it backwards (6 minutes).** Take the two biggest clusters. For each, the group answers three questions in order: what was true the week before this happened, what was true the month before, and what was true on the day we made this plan. Keep going until you land on something that is true right now, today, in this room.

5. **One of three (4 minutes).** For each present-tense fact you landed on, the group picks exactly one of: accept it and move on, watch it and name the signal that would tell us it is happening, or change the plan today. Every fact gets a disposition. No fact gets left undecided.

**Facilitator Tips:**
- Step 2 works because it is specific and a bit funny. If people write vague corporate failure, ask them to write it the way they would actually message a colleague
- The backwards walk is where groups want to stop early, usually at "we did not have enough time." Push for the next step back. The useful answer is almost always further down
- Three options only. Given four, groups invent a fifth that means nothing happens

**Variations:** For politically loaded plans, collect step 2 anonymously and have the facilitator read them out. For a fast version, run steps 1 to 3 only and take the clusters into a separate decision meeting

**Source:** the premortem technique, developed by Gary Klein and first published as "Performing a Project Premortem" in Harvard Business Review, 2007. This version's adaptations are the internal-announcement headline framing in step 2, the clustering in step 3, the time-sequenced backward causal walk in step 4, and the forced accept, watch, or change close in step 5. Write-up consulted: https://gary-klein.com/premortem

### 6. Three Things on a Card

**Purpose:** Get a room of strangers connected in a way they still remember at the end, instead of six forgettable introductions.

**Group Size:** 15-80
**Time:** 20 minutes
**Materials:** Blank cards and lanyards or pins, markers

**Step-by-Step Facilitation:**

1. **Fill the card (3 minutes).** Three things, no job title: something you know a lot about, something you are currently bad at, and a place you have lived. Wear it where people can read it.

2. **Go and find the overlap (10 minutes).** No timer, no bell, no assigned partners. One instruction: find two people whose card connects to yours somehow, and work out how. That is the whole brief.

3. **Write the back of your own card (4 minutes).** Sit down. Everyone writes one sentence on the back of their own card: "the person I want to talk to again is ___, because ___."

4. **Say the name (3 minutes).** Round the room. Read only the name and the because. The person you name is sitting right there, hearing it in public. That is the part people remember.

**Facilitator Tips:**
- The "currently bad at" line does most of the work. People connect over it far faster than over expertise, and it gives shy people something safe to lead with
- Resist adding a timer to step 2. The lack of a bell is what makes it feel like a conversation instead of an exercise
- If the room is over 60, split step 4 into table groups so nobody waits fifteen minutes for their turn

**Variations:** Swap the third field for something local to your context ("a job you had before this one" works well). For remote sessions, cards become a shared board and step 4 happens in the chat with names tagged

**Source:** Klara's original design.

### 7. What Happens When

**Purpose:** Build the small set of working agreements a group actually needs, by starting from the friction that is coming rather than from a list of rules.

**Group Size:** 5-25
**Time:** 22 minutes
**Materials:** Sticky notes, wall, dot stickers

**Step-by-Step Facilitation:**

1. **Write the friction (5 minutes).** In pairs, write scenarios that all start with the same three words: "what happens when." Real moments, not principles. What happens when someone misses a deadline and does not say so. What happens when two people disagree and neither will move. What happens when someone goes quiet for two weeks. What happens when a decision gets made without you in the room.

2. **Pick four (4 minutes).** Post them all. Read them. Everyone gets three dots for the ones most likely to happen to this group in the next three months. No discussion during voting, it only anchors people.

3. **Who moves first (10 minutes).** For each of the top four, the group answers one question and only one: who does what, first? Not a value, not a principle, a first move by a named role. Write each as a single sentence.

4. **The absent-person test (3 minutes).** Read the four sentences back. If any of them depends on someone who is not in this room, it does not count. Rewrite it as something this group can do on its own.

**Facilitator Tips:**
- Groups will drift toward writing values ("we communicate openly"). Ask them what the first move is, and keep asking until you get a person and a verb
- Four is the cap. A group that can genuinely run four agreements is far ahead of one that wrote twelve
- Put the four sentences somewhere visible and revisit them the first time one of the scenarios actually happens. That is the moment they either become real or get quietly dropped

**Variations:** For a new team, run it in week one and again at week six with the same scenarios. For remote groups, use a shared board and vote with emoji

**Source:** Klara's original design.

### 8. The Question Wall

**Purpose:** Get at what an expert actually knows, including the edges of it, rather than the polished version they prepared.

**Group Size:** 20-200, one guest
**Time:** 35-40 minutes
**Materials:** Sticky notes, a large wall or three flipcharts, markers

**Step-by-Step Facilitation:**

1. **Questions before the guest (5 minutes, or collect in advance).** Everyone writes questions and puts them on the wall. No grouping, no curation. The guest is out of the room or turned away for this.

2. **The sort (3 minutes).** The guest comes to the wall and sorts every question into three columns, out loud, while the room watches: "I can answer this in one sentence," "I have a real story about this one," and "I do not know." Doing this aloud is the method. The room learns the shape of the expertise before hearing any of it, and the third column earns more trust than any answer will.

3. **Work the middle column (20 minutes).** The guest takes the story questions in whatever order they like, standing at the wall. No host, no chairs, no interviewer. They move a note as they finish it so the room can see the pile going down.

4. **Rapid fire (5 minutes).** The one-sentence column, at speed, one sentence each. No elaboration allowed, and the room is invited to hold them to it. It gets funny, which is fine.

5. **Give away the last column (5 minutes).** The "I do not know" notes go back to the room. Anyone with a partial answer speaks. Somebody usually does, and it changes the register from performance to shared problem.

**Facilitator Tips:**
- Brief the guest properly beforehand. Some people find the "I do not know" column frightening until they see how the room responds to it, which is almost always with more respect, not less
- Do not let a host creep back in. The moment someone starts interviewing, you are running a panel again
- If the wall is thin, seed a few questions yourself in advance so the sort has something to work with

**Variations:** Two guests sorting the same wall separately is revealing, especially where they disagree about which column a question belongs in. For remote sessions, use a shared board with three columns and have the guest drag notes while narrating

**Source:** Klara's original design.

## WORLD CAFÉ

**Purpose:** Host meaningful conversations moving beyond small talk. Harvest collective intelligence. Enable cross-pollination of ideas.

**Group Size:** 10-40 optimal, 4-6 per table
**Time:** 60-120 minutes (15-20 min setup + 60 min conversations + 15-30 min harvest)
**Materials:** Round tables covered with paper, colored markers, refreshments, flowers, optional talking stick, flipchart for harvest, comfortable chairs

**Step-by-Step Facilitation:**

1. **Setup (20 minutes before)** — Arrange tables scattered (not rows). Cover with white paper. Place markers, flowers, refreshments at each table. Set welcoming café atmosphere.

2. **Opening & Welcome (5-10 minutes)** — Welcome group. Introduce topic. Explain World Café process. Explain table host role. Invite informal seating at tables (4-6 per table).

3. **Round 1 (20 minutes)** — Groups discuss first question (same for all or varied). Table host supports flow without leading. One person records in words and drawings on table paper. Participants listen to understand.

4. **Round 1 Transition (2 minutes)** — Ring bell. One person stays as table host. All others stand and move to new table.

5. **Round 2 (20 minutes)** — Repeat at new tables. Table host brings incoming group up to speed. Same or deepening question.

6. **Round 2 Transition (2 minutes)** — Ring bell. Form new pairs again.

7. **Round 3 (20 minutes)** — Final round. Question can be synthesis or action question. Many will know multiple people.

8. **Harvesting (15-30 minutes)** — Reconvene whole group. Invite participants to share key insights, themes, patterns, questions that emerged across tables. Facilitator or recorder synthesizes on flipchart. Each table shares few key insights.

**Question Design for Rounds:**
- **Round 1:** Open, generative, personal connection ("What drew you to this conversation?")
- **Round 2:** Deepening ("What new insights are you having?")
- **Round 3:** Action or synthesis ("What possibilities are we creating together?")

**Table Host Role:**
- Welcome incoming participants warmly
- Briefly summarize previous round (1-2 minutes)
- Do NOT direct or lead the new conversation
- Support flow by asking follow-up if silence emerges
- Encourage all voices
- Note key themes on the paper

**Facilitator Tips:**
- Design compelling, open-ended questions
- Use café ambiance to create informality and comfort
- Ring transitions clearly but gently
- Position as orchestrator, not expert
- In harvesting, look for patterns and tensions
- Invite participants to look at table paper drawings and notes
- Create psychological safety in opening

**Variations:** Fishbowl World Café, Standing Café, Progressive question sequence, Stakeholder worlds, Virtual World Café

**Source:** The World Café format was originated by Juanita Brown and David Isaacs; the authoritative material is at https://www.theworldcafe.com. The description above is this skill's own; write-up consulted: https://www.sessionlab.com/methods/world-cafe

## OPEN SPACE TECHNOLOGY

Open Space Technology is a self-organizing format for large groups working on a complex issue where nobody can predetermine the outcome. Participants propose the sessions themselves at the start, publish them on a shared agenda wall, and then choose which to attend. It was developed by Harrison Owen in the mid-1980s.

**Group Size:** 5 to 1,500+, with 50-150 the range where it works best
**Time:** Half a day at minimum, and it gets better with more

Open Space runs on a small set of named principles plus one guiding rule about participants moving freely between sessions. We do not reproduce that wording here, because it is the heart of the method and the source states it better than a paraphrase would. Read it at the source before you facilitate this, and read it properly rather than skimming it, since the principles do the work that a facilitator would otherwise have to do by hand.

**When to reach for it:** a genuinely open question, a room with real diversity of stake and view, enough time for people to follow their own interest, and an organizer willing to give up control of the agenda. If any of those four is missing, run something else. A pre-cooked Open Space is worse than an honest structured agenda, and participants can always tell.

**What it asks of you as facilitator:** less than you expect during the sessions themselves, and more than you expect in the framing at the start and the harvest at the end. The format holds the middle. Your work is the opening circle, the marketplace where topics get posted, and making sure the notes from each session actually get collected and sent out afterwards.

**Related formats in this skill:** unconferences and BarCamp as event wrappers around Open Space are covered in `hackathons-and-builds.md`.

**Source:** Harrison Owen and the Open Space Institute. Full principles, the guiding rule, and the complete facilitation sequence: https://openspaceworld.org/wp2/what-is/

## FISHBOWL DISCUSSION

**Purpose:** Develop active listening and observation skills. Engage meaningful conversations while observers learn. Deepen understanding through role-switching.

**Group Size:** 10-20 optimal, Inner circle 4-6, Outer circle remainder
**Time:** 45-60 minutes (15 min round 1 + 5 min swap + 15 min round 2 + 10-15 min debrief)
**Materials:** Chairs in two concentric circles, Observer handouts (optional), Pens, Flipchart (optional), 3-5 prepared discussion questions

**Step-by-Step Facilitation:**

1. **Preparation** — Design 3-5 guiding questions. Identify diverse inner circle participants. Prepare observer handouts (optional). Brief inner circle on roles.

2. **Setup (10 minutes)** — Arrange chairs in two concentric circles. Inner: 4-6 chairs facing each other. Outer: remaining chairs to see and hear clearly. Display prepared questions visibly. Ask for volunteers or assign to inner circle (mix perspectives).

3. **Introduction (3-5 minutes)** — Welcome. Explain structure. Introduce topic and questions. Set norms: "Inner circle: talk to each other, not the outer circle. Use specific examples. Outer circle: listen carefully, take notes on discussion content, tone, and patterns."

4. **Round 1 Discussion (15 minutes)** — Inner circle discusses using prepared questions. Facilitator may ask clarifying questions if discussion stalls. Outer circle observes silently and takes notes. Facilitator enforces "no speeches" rule—keep contributions conversational.

5. **Role Swap (5 minutes)** — Ring bell. Inner circle moves to outer. Outer moves to inner. Brief debrief: "Inner circle, think about what you were experiencing. Outer circle, you're entering now."

6. **Round 2 Discussion (15 minutes)** — New inner circle discusses same or new deepening question. Repeat conversation structure. New outer circle observes.

7. **Whole Group Debrief (10-15 minutes)** — Reconvene in whole group or semi-circle. Ask reflection questions: "What patterns did you notice?" "What surprised you?" "What was it like to observe vs. participate?" Invite volunteer sharing.

**Discussion Facilitation Moves (for inner circle):**
- "Can you give a specific example?"
- "Tell us more about that."
- "How does that connect to what [other person] just said?"
- "What evidence supports that?"
- "What would need to be true for that to work?"
- **STOP over-explanation:** "That's helpful; let's hear from someone else"

**Debrief Questions:**
- "What did you notice about the conversation?"
- "What themes or patterns emerged?"
- "What was challenging about listening without talking?"
- "How did it feel to be in the inner circle?"
- "What did observing teach you about this topic?"

**Variations:** Open Fishbowl (one empty chair that outer circle can enter), Rotating Fishbowl, Fishbowl with chat, Multi-round, Goldfish Bowl (standing, tighter circle)

**Facilitator Tips:**
- Set norms clearly
- Enforce focus; no speeches
- Manage dominators gently
- Ask for evidence
- Surface disagreement
- Note patterns for debrief
- Create psychological safety

**Source:** Fishbowl is a long-standing format in general facilitation practice with no single owner. The description above is this skill's own; write-up consulted: https://www.sessionlab.com/methods/fishbowl

## MASTERMIND GROUPS

Small group of like-minded people committed to supporting each other's growth, problem-solving, and success. Members serve as both advisors and accountable partners.

**Purpose:** Provide peer coaching and collaborative problem-solving. Build accountability on goals. Access diverse expertise. Create container for vulnerability.

**Group Size:** 4-6 ideal, 3 minimum, 8-10 maximum
**Duration:** Long-term relationships (3-12 months minimum)
**Frequency:** Monthly or bi-weekly standing commitments
**Time Per Session:** 90 minutes typical
**Per Person's "Hot Seat":** 20-30 minutes focused time
**Materials:** Round table or circle of comfortable chairs, Timer, Optional: Notebook for recording commitments, Shared document for tracking action items, Refreshments

**Typical 90-Minute Session Structure:**

| Phase | Time | Action |
|-------|------|--------|
| **CHECK-IN** | 5 min | Brief opening. Everyone: "What are you bringing today?" (1 sentence each) |
| **PERSON 1 HOT SEAT** | 25 min | 5 min share challenge/goal, 15 min peer advisory, 5 min commitment and next steps |
| **BREAK** | 5 min | Move, refresh |
| **PERSON 2 HOT SEAT** | 25 min | Same as above |
| **BREAK** | 5 min | (Optional) |
| **PERSON 3 HOT SEAT** | 25 min | Same as above |
| **CHECK-OUT** | 5 min | "One commitment I'm making" or "One thing I'm taking forward" |

**Pre-Group Setup:**
1. Recruit 4-6 aligned people (similar stage, shared challenges, growth mindset)
2. Set expectations clearly: frequency, duration, confidentiality, commitment, cost (if any)
3. Schedule recurring sessions in advance
4. Create shared document for tracking commitments

**Core Facilitation: The Hot Seat Rounds**

**PERSON IN HOT SEAT (5 minutes: Sharing Challenge/Goal):**
- "What's on your mind? What do you need support with?"
- Share specific challenge, goal, or decision point
- Be vulnerable and honest
- Group listens without interruption
- Group refrains from solutions in this phase
- This person owns the problem; others are witnesses

**ADVISORS' TURN (15 minutes: Peer Advisory):**
- Group members ask clarifying questions first:
  - "What have you already tried?"
  - "What's your desired outcome?"
  - "What's the real fear underneath?"
- Then shift to:
  - Offering reflections: "What I'm hearing is..."
  - Sharing relevant experiences: "In my situation, I..."
  - Proposing ideas: "What if you tried...?"
- Advisor's Job:
  - Listen for what's not being said
  - Ask questions that deepen thinking
  - Share hard truths with compassion
  - Offer perspective from outside the situation
  - Honor the person's autonomy and choice

**HOT SEAT PERSON'S TURN (5 minutes: Commitments & Next Steps):**
- "Based on this conversation, what are you committing to?"
- Make specific, measurable commitments
- Share by when: "By our next meeting, I will..."
- Others hold them accountable

**FULL CYCLE:**
- Repeat for each person until all have had hot seat time
- Keep timing sacred (use timer visibly)

**Facilitator Tips:**

**For Advisors:**
- Ask questions, not statements
- Avoid "fixing" the person's problem
- Share experiences, not prescriptions
- Notice patterns they might not see
- Hold both compassion and candor
- Speak as a peer, not an expert

**For Hot Seat Person:**
- Be specific about what you need
- Listen to understand, not to defend
- Ask for what would help most
- Make commitments you'll keep
- Report back on progress at next meeting

**For Facilitator/Group:**
- Keep time sacred—timer visible
- Protect equal speaking time
- Manage dominators gently
- Track commitments and follow up
- Celebrate wins and progress
- Refresh group agreements periodically

**Variations:** Quarterly deep dives, Topic-based meetings, Expert guests, Cohort model, Peer mentoring, Accountability checkpoints

**Source:** The mastermind-group concept originates with Napoleon Hill, *Think and Grow Rich* (1937). The description above is this skill's own; write-up consulted: https://voltagecontrol.com/events/facilitation-lab-facilitating-mastermind-conversations/

## SHOW AND TELL / DEMO SESSIONS

Get deeper understanding of stakeholders' perspectives through object-based sharing.

**Purpose:** Understand perspectives via metaphor and narrative. Build empathy across teams. Celebrate work in progress. Create safe space for emotional expression.

**Group Size:** 2-15 optimal (5-8 for intimacy)
**Time:** 15-30 minutes total (2-3 min per person + group response time)
**Materials:** Objects brought by participants, Whiteboard/flipchart and markers, Comfortable seating in circle, Quiet, safe space

**Step-by-Step Facilitation:**

1. **Preparation (Before session)** — Invite: "Bring an object that represents your perspective on [topic]." Keep objects hidden. Set expectation: "2-3 minutes to show and explain. All perspectives valued."

2. **Set the Stage (2-3 minutes)** — Welcome. Display topic/prompt on whiteboard. Optional: sketch visual representations. Explain: "Each person shows their object and explains why. Our job is listen with curiosity. There are no right or wrong objects."

3. **Model Vulnerability (optional 2-3 minutes)** — If appropriate, facilitator shares own object first. Significantly increases safety and participation.

4. **Invite Volunteers (2-3 minutes per person)** — Ask: "Who'd like to share first?" Each person: Takes out object, shows it, explains why they chose it, describes what it represents about their perspective. Keep to 2-3 minutes. Listen intently. Take visual notes or sketches as they speak.

5. **Synthesize Themes (5-10 minutes)** — After all shared, facilitator summarizes on whiteboard: "Here are the themes I'm noticing..." (excitement, concerns, doubt, uncertainty, possibility, etc.). Ask: "What patterns do you see?"

6. **Deepen Understanding (5-10 minutes)** — Ask follow-up questions: "What surprised you?" "What connections do you see?" "What shifted for you?" Invite people to notice what objects were chosen and by whom.

**Active Listening & Documentation:**
- Listen for emotional language, hesitations, values, concerns, hopes
- Note similarities, differences, surprising elements
- Sketch simple visual representations alongside notes
- Summarize back: "It sounds like... Is that right?"

**Variations:** Structured feedback ("I like, I wish, What if?"), Gallery walk, Metaphor extended, Artifact swap, Digital version with photos and explanations

**Feedback Format: "I Like, I Wish, What If?"**
- **I Like:** "I like that you chose something representing hope..."
- **I Wish:** "I wish everyone felt that level of confidence..."
- **What If:** "What if we used this as a guiding image for our project?"

**Facilitator Tips:**
- Model vulnerability; share your object first if possible
- Maintain silence after someone shares for reflection
- Ask follow-up questions
- Validate all contributions
- Synthesize across contributions visibly
- Honor emotional content
- Document visually

**Source:** Show and tell is general practice with no single owner. The description above is this skill's own; write-up consulted: https://www.sessionlab.com/methods/show-and-tell

## MEANINGFUL CHECK-INS AND CHECK-OUTS

Rituals that open and close sessions with purpose. Create presence, build connection, enable reflection without forcing lengthy processing.

**Purpose:** Check-ins establish presence, focus, group commitment at opening. Check-outs enable symbolic closure and personal reflection at closing. Build connection and psychological safety.

**Group Size:** 2-40 participants
**Time:** 5-30 minutes for check-in, 10-20 minutes for check-out (varies by group size and question depth)
**Materials:** Chairs in circle, Optional: talking stick, Optional: flipchart, Optional: music

**Alternative Check-In Questions:**

**Scaled Questions:**
- "On a scale of 1-10, how present are you today?"
- "Rate your energy from low to high"
- "How aligned are you with the purpose of this meeting?"

**Weather/Temperature Questions:**
- "If today were a weather forecast for your mood, what would it be?"
- "What temperature are you today and why?"
- "If you were a season, which would you be right now?"

**Object-Based Questions:**
- "Describe your personality using three unrelated objects"
- "What object from this room represents how you're feeling?"
- "Choose one word that describes your energy today"

**Personal Connection Questions:**
- "What are you grateful for right now?"
- "What's one thing that made you smile recently?"
- "Who inspires you and why?"

**Work-Related Check-Ins:**
- "What's your intention for this session?"
- "What question are you holding?"
- "What brought you here today?"
- "What do you hope to take away from this time together?"

**Playful/Energizing Check-Ins:**
- "One word that describes your mood?"
- "Emoji that captures your vibe?"
- "Animal spirit that matches your energy?"
- "Song that's stuck in your head?"

**Check-Out Question Options:**

**Reflection-Based:**
- "One insight you're taking with you?"
- "One commitment you're making?"
- "What will you do differently based on this session?"
- "What's one thing you'll remember?"

**Gratitude-Based:**
- "Who or what are you grateful for from this session?"
- "What will you appreciate about today?"
- "One compliment for the group?"

**Integration-Based:**
- "How will you use what you learned?"
- "What's next for you?"
- "One action you'll take this week?"

**Poetic:**
- "Complete this: 'I came seeking ___; I'm leaving with ___'"
- "If this session were a plant, it would be..."
- "One word that will remind you of today?"

**Step-by-Step Facilitation for Check-In:**

1. **Framing (1-2 minutes)** — Invite circle. Explain: "We're opening by checking in. Chance to be present and connect. Here's the question..." Clarify: "Share whatever feels true. You can pass. Aim for 1-2 sentences."

2. **Optional Object Pass (0 minutes)** — If using, place meaningful object in center. Explain: "When you're speaking, hold this. It's our talking piece."

3. **Going Around Circle (varies)** — Each person shares in order (or randomly). Keep to 1-2 sentences. Listen with full attention. Allow passes. Keep momentum.

4. **Transition to Work (1 minute)** — Brief summary: "I hear excitement, some uncertainty, commitment. Let's carry that into our time together." Move into substantive work.

**Step-by-Step Facilitation for Check-Out:**

1. **Transition (1-2 minutes)** — Pause work 10-15 minutes before end. Invite circle. Explain: "Chance to reflect on what we're taking with us."

2. **Offer Question (0 minutes)** — Pose check-out question. Example: "One word that captures what you're leaving with?"

3. **Going Around Circle (varies)** — Each person shares (1-2 sentences max). Full attention. Listen for wisdom and closure. Allow passes. Celebrate insights briefly.

4. **Closing Acknowledgment (1-2 minutes)** — Optional: "Thank you for being present. The work we did here matters. Go forward with..."

**Facilitator Tips:**

**General:**
- Model brevity: go first with example
- "You can pass at any time"
- Full attention; put away phones
- Allow silence; don't rush gaps
- Validate all contributions
- Notice patterns (what themes emerge?)

**For Check-Ins:**
- Keep energy light
- Choose questions aligned with session purpose
- Use playful questions for virtual meetings
- Save deepest questions for mid-session

**For Check-Outs:**
- Allow more reflection time at end
- Offer choice: "One word or one sentence"
- Capture on flipchart if appropriate
- Honor silence; people need time
- Celebrate wins and learning

**Variations (for large groups 20+):**
- Pair/share: People turn to neighbor, then one shares to larger group
- Small group circles: Break into 3-4 circles; simultaneous check-in/out
- Silent reflection: People write on card; facilitator reads key ones
- Popcorn style: Anyone can share when ready (not forced order)

**For Virtual/Hybrid:**
- Chat check-in: People type; facilitator reads aloud
- Breakout rooms: Small groups simultaneously
- Emoji reactions: Use reaction buttons for mood
- Shared doc: Type responses while facilitator reads some aloud
- Hybrid option: In-person speak first, then each remote person
- Visual check-in: People hold up fingers (1-10 energy); no words

**Source:** Opening and closing rounds are general facilitation practice. The description above is this skill's own; write-up consulted: https://hyperisland.com/en/toolbox/check-in-check-out

## MEETING & TEAM METHODS

Three of this skill's own designs for the recurring jobs inside teams: opening a session, looking honestly at how the team works, and teaching iteration. The other two, One Moment Each and The Middle First, adapt established formats credited in their own Source lines below. Two large public libraries cover this ground with their own methods and their own wording, and they are worth going to directly rather than reading a summary: the Hyper Island Toolbox (toolbox.hyperisland.com) and SessionLab (sessionlab.com), whose write-up on the human spectrogram informed The Middle First's adaptation.

### 15. The Opening Contract

**Purpose:** Start a session so that everyone knows why they are there, what their input actually does, and when they get their time back.

**Group Size:** Any
**Time:** 3 minutes at the open, 3 minutes at the close
**Materials:** Somewhere visible to write four lines

**Step-by-Step Facilitation:**

1. **What we are deciding, or not deciding (30 seconds).** Say it plainly, and the negative version counts: "by the end of this hour we will have picked one of these three options," or equally, "we are not deciding anything today, we are gathering input for a decision Priya makes on Friday." Both are fine. Ambiguity is not.

2. **What your input does (45 seconds).** The honest version. Who decides, and how much weight the room's view carries. This is the sentence most agendas leave out, and it is the one that determines whether people bother contributing. If the decision is already made, say that, and explain why you still want them in the room. People forgive a made decision. They do not forgive being consulted for show.

3. **When you get your time back (15 seconds).** An end time, and what happens if you run over. "We finish at 3:30. If we are not done, we stop anyway and I will send round what is left."

4. **The room's line (90 seconds).** Round the group. Each person says, in under ten seconds, what would need to have happened for this to have been worth their time. Write them where everyone can see.

5. **Read them back (3 minutes, at the close).** Before people leave, read the lines from step 4 and ask which got met. The ones that did not are your agenda for next time, and saying so out loud is the part that makes people believe step 4 was real.

**Facilitator Tips:**
- Step 2 is the one people skip because it is uncomfortable. It is also the entire value of the tool. Practise saying the awkward version out loud before the session
- Keep step 4 to ten seconds a person or a room of twenty will eat your whole opening. Cut people off warmly and they will not mind
- Do not skip step 5 because you are running late. Skipping it teaches the room that step 4 was theatre, and next time they will give you nothing

**Variations:** For recurring meetings, keep the same four lines on a standing card and only re-do steps 1 and 4. For large rooms, take step 4 from a sample of six people rather than everyone. Written asset version: `assets/opening-contract-template.md`

**Source:** Klara's original design. Other tools work this territory, including the six-field IDOARRT worksheet in the Hyper Island Toolbox, which is worth a look if you want a fuller pre-meeting checklist: https://toolbox.hyperisland.com

### 16. One Moment Each

**Purpose:** Peer feedback that is specific enough to use, without turning into either vague praise or an ambush.

**Group Size:** 4-10, people who have worked together for a while
**Time:** 20 minutes
**Materials:** Slips of paper, enough for each person to write one per colleague

**Step-by-Step Facilitation:**

1. **Explain the shape (2 minutes).** Every slip contains two things and nothing else: one specific occasion, and its effect on you. "In the Tuesday review, when you re-asked the question three different ways, I finally understood what we were actually arguing about." No advice. No suggestions. No "you should."

2. **Write (8 minutes).** One slip per colleague. If you cannot think of a specific occasion for someone, write that you could not, and hand it over anyway. That is honest and it is useful.

3. **Read your own, silently (3 minutes).** Everyone gets their slips and reads them alone. No talking. Let this be genuinely quiet.

4. **The surprising one (6 minutes).** Each person picks the slip that surprised them most and reads it aloud, then says only why it surprised them. Not whether they agree. Not what they will do about it. Just why they did not expect it.

5. **One line for yourself (1 minute).** Everyone writes one sentence, not shared with anyone: "the thing I now know people see."

**Facilitator Tips:**
- The no-advice rule feels restrictive and it is the reason this works. Advice makes people defensive; a described effect does not
- "Why it surprised me" is a much safer public share than "what I will change," and it produces more honest reflection anyway
- If someone gets a slip that stings, do not intervene publicly. Check in with them afterwards

**Variations:** Run it before a longer retrospective to warm the room. For distributed teams, collect slips through a form and distribute privately, then hold steps 4 and 5 on a call

**Source:** the Situation-Behavior-Impact (SBI) feedback model, developed by the Center for Creative Leadership. This version's adaptations are the group slip-writing in step 2, the private silent reading in step 3, and the closing round built around the slip that surprised each person in step 4. Write-up consulted: https://www.ccl.org/articles/leading-effectively-articles/closing-the-gap-between-intent-vs-impact-sbii/

### 17. The Gap Round

**Purpose:** Surface what the team already knows about how it works but has not said out loud.

**Group Size:** 5-15
**Time:** 25 minutes
**Materials:** Two differently coloured cards per person

**Step-by-Step Facilitation:**

1. **Two questions, written privately (5 minutes).** On the first card: what is the thing about how we work that you think most people here would name as the problem? On the second: what is the thing you would name, that you think nobody else here would?

2. **Collect the stacks separately (1 minute).** Keep them apart. The whole method depends on reading them in order.

3. **Read the first stack (6 minutes).** Aloud, all of them. There is usually heavy overlap, and the room sees plainly that it has a shared, known, unaddressed problem. Let that sit for a moment before moving on. Nobody needs to comment.

4. **Read the second stack (8 minutes).** This is the one. Frequently three or four people have written versions of the same thing they each believed only they thought. Watch the room when that lands, because that is the moment the conversation actually changes.

5. **Pick one (5 minutes).** From either stack, the team picks a single item it will do something about before the next meeting, and names who makes the first move. One item. Not a list.

**Facilitator Tips:**
- Read the stacks in order and do not merge them. Reading the second stack first loses the effect entirely
- Read every card, including the ones that repeat. The repetition is the data
- If the second stack turns out to contain something about a person in the room, slow down and handle it as a conversation, not as an agenda item

**Variations:** Run quarterly with the same two questions and keep the cards, so the team can see what moved. For a nervous group, have the facilitator transcribe and read all cards so handwriting is not identifiable

**Source:** Klara's original design.

### 18. Four Showings

**Purpose:** Teach, by having people feel it, that early and rough beats late and polished.

**Group Size:** 8-40, in teams of four
**Time:** 25 minutes
**Materials:** Paper and markers, a visible timer

**Step-by-Step Facilitation:**

1. **The brief (2 minutes).** Give every team the same small concrete task. Something like: design a poster that would make a stranger come to your event. Or: name a new team ritual and write the one line that explains it. It has to be makeable, not discussable.

2. **The schedule, announced up front (1 minute).** You will show a version to another team at minute 3, minute 6, minute 9 and minute 12. Four showings, non-negotiable. Say this before they start, and then say nothing else about strategy.

3. **Run it (12 minutes).** At each showing, the receiving team gives exactly one sentence, always the same shape: "the thing I do not get is..." No praise, no suggestions, no questions. Then straight back to work.

4. **The debrief (10 minutes).** Two questions. Which version changed the most, and what caused it? And: what were you doing between minute 0 and minute 3? Almost every group has at least one team that spent the first three minutes discussing and arrived at showing one with nothing, losing their first round of feedback. Let them notice it themselves rather than telling them.

**Facilitator Tips:**
- Do not warn them that discussion will cost them the first showing. The lesson only lands if they walk into it
- Hold the one-sentence response format strictly. The moment it becomes advice, teams start defending instead of changing
- Run the timer visibly and call showings loudly. The pressure is the pedagogy

**Variations:** Six showings at two-minute intervals for a more brutal version. For remote sessions, teams post to a shared board and comment on the team below them

**Source:** Klara's original design. The most famous activity teaching this lesson is the Marshmallow Challenge, popularised by Tom Wujec, which uses spaghetti and tape and is worth running if you have the materials and the space: https://www.tomwujec.com/marshmallowchallenge

### 19. The Middle First

**Purpose:** Get a room to show where it stands physically, and then actually move, rather than positioning and defending.

**Group Size:** 10-100
**Time:** 12 minutes per statement
**Materials:** Floor space, an imaginary line, tape optional

**Step-by-Step Facilitation:**

1. **Read the statement (1 minute).** One clear statement people can genuinely disagree about. Point to the two ends of the line and name them. Everyone positions themselves.

2. **Interview the middle first (4 minutes).** Not the ends. Take three people standing near the middle and ask each for one sentence on what makes it hard to pick. The undecided are holding both sides at once and almost nobody ever asks them, so this is the freshest thinking in the room and it sets a tone of thinking rather than arguing.

3. **The ends, with a specific job (4 minutes).** Thirty seconds each from two or three people at each end. Their job is not to argue their case. It is to finish this sentence: "I would move from here if it turned out that..."

4. **Read it again and move (3 minutes).** Same statement, second positioning. Anyone whose view shifted, move. Movement is the only output that matters here. Ask one person who moved what did it.

**Facilitator Tips:**
- Interviewing the middle first is the whole design. Start at the ends and you get two speeches and nobody moves
- "I would move if it turned out that" is a genuinely different question from "why do you think that," and it is what makes step 4 produce anything
- Watch for senior people positioning early and the room drifting toward them. If that is a risk, have everyone close their eyes and step, then open

**Variations:** For sensitive topics, run it with anonymous cards on a taped scale instead of bodies. For remote sessions, use a slider poll, show the distribution, then re-poll after step 3 and show both

**Source:** the human spectrogram, an established facilitation format for showing where a room stands on a line. This version's adaptations are interviewing the middle first and the "I would move if it turned out that" prompt for the ends. Write-up consulted: https://www.sessionlab.com/methods/human-spectrogram

## WORKSHOP DESIGN FOR HANDS-ON LEARNING

Hands-on workshops teach a skill or build something concrete in a short window. They sit at the intersection of teaching and doing: too much demo and people stay passive, too little structure and they get lost. This section covers workshop design (especially for AI and coding work), pair and mob programming formats, and show & tell closing structures.

### Workshop Design for Build Sessions

#### Structure & Duration

**Standard format**:
- 3-4 hour intensive session
- Small groups (8-20 participants optimal)
- Mix of demo, guided practice, and independent building

**Compressed format** (for hackathons):
- 90-minute deep dive
- Focus on specific use case or tool
- Participants bring own projects to apply learning

#### Key Components

1. **Problem context** (10 min)
   - Real-world problem you're solving
   - Why this tool or approach helps
   - Expected outcomes

2. **Tool walkthrough** (15-20 min)
   - Demo of the specific tool/API
   - Show 2-3 practical examples
   - Demonstrate common pitfalls and how to avoid them

3. **Guided practice** (15-20 min)
   - Participants follow along
   - Build simple first example together
   - Instructor provides step-by-step guidance
   - Shared code repository or starter template

4. **Hands-on building** (30-45 min)
   - Participants build their own projects
   - Apply tool to their use case or problem
   - Peer collaboration encouraged
   - Instructor circulates for support

5. **Sharing & reflection** (10-15 min)
   - Show and tell of what people built
   - Share learning insights
   - Discuss what worked and challenges
   - Distribute resources for continued learning

#### Resource Preparation

**Before the workshop**:
- Create starter template or boilerplate code
- Prepare working examples
- Test all tools and APIs beforehand
- Have backup internet/API solutions
- Create detailed documentation (in-workshop and after)
- Prepare troubleshooting guide for common errors

**During the workshop**:
- Provide printed cheat sheets
- Share code repository link
- Screen-share demos clearly
- Have sample projects for people to fork
- Set expectations for learning pace

### Pair & Mob Programming Formats

#### Pair Programming

**Definition**: Two developers work together at one workstation on a single task, with defined roles:

- **Driver**: Types code, implements logic
- **Navigator**: Reviews code as it's written, spots errors, provides feedback, suggests improvements
- **Rotation**: Partners swap roles regularly (every 20-30 minutes)

**Benefits**:
- Significantly improves code quality through immediate review
- Increases knowledge sharing and mentoring
- Reduces design mistakes before they become expensive
- Builds camaraderie and shared ownership

**Best practices**:
- Set timer for role rotation
- Both partners stay engaged and present
- Create safe space for questions and suggestions
- Pairing works best with one person more experienced than the other
- Pair junior developers with more experienced ones

**With AI assistance**:
- Use AI tools (ChatGPT, GitHub Copilot, Claude) as third "voice" in pair
- AI can generate code suggestions; pair decides whether to accept
- Human pair maintains control of direction and quality
- Pair debugging and validating AI-generated code together
- Use AI for research and documentation lookup

#### Ensemble (Mob) Programming

**Definition**: Entire team works together on a single codebase, with everyone contributing to the same screen/shared workspace.

**Roles**:
- **Driver**: Implements current task, types code
- **Navigators**: Provide direction and strategy
- **Ensemble**: Everyone contributes thinking and ideas

**Format**:
- All team members visible on video call
- Single shared screen (driver broadcasts)
- Typed pseudocode or outline before implementation
- Full group discusses approach before coding

**Benefits**:
- Highest knowledge sharing
- Faster learning and skill development
- Reduced defects (continuous review)
- Strong team cohesion
- Faster onboarding of new team members

**With AI**:
- Use AI to generate test cases while team codes implementation
- AI refactors code suggestions from human ensemble
- Use AI for rapid documentation of ensemble's decisions
- AI assists with edge case and error handling thinking

#### Show & Tell Format

**Purpose**: Participants demonstrate what they built and what they learned.

**Structure**:
- 2-5 minutes per person/team
- Show the working prototype
- Explain key learning or technical choice
- Briefly discuss challenges and solutions
- Audience asks questions

**Best practices**:
- Set clear time limits
- Encourage questions
- Create psychological safety (all attempts valued)
- Record shows (participants and asynchronous viewers)
- Give positive feedback to all presenters
- Document learnings across all presentations

**Source:** Adapted from hackathon and build-event organizer practices.

## THE COMMUNITY QUICK READ

**Purpose:** Work out fast, and honestly, whether a community is worth building, before anyone commits months to it. Klara's own tool. Full prompts live in `assets/community-quick-read.md`.

**Group Size:** 3-5 core stakeholders
**Time:** 30-40 minutes
**Materials:** `assets/community-quick-read.md`, something to capture answers with

**Step-by-Step Facilitation:**

1. **Frame it (2 minutes).** Say plainly that the job here is to find out whether this should exist, and that "no" is a legitimate and useful answer. Groups that skip this line spend forty minutes justifying instead of checking.

2. **Round one: is it already happening (10 minutes).** Each person names one real instance from the last month of two people in this group helping each other without anyone organizing it. Real names, real occasions. If the group cannot produce three between them, stop and say so out loud. That is the finding.

3. **Round two: the cost of joining (12 minutes).** Count what it actually costs a member to get to their first useful interaction, in minutes and in social risk. Then name what they get in that same sitting. If the cost lands before the benefit, you have found the design problem.

4. **Round three: who does it when you are ill (10 minutes).** Name the person, not the role, who keeps it running for two weeks if the convener disappears. No name means this is a service one person delivers, which is a fine thing to be, but it should be a decision rather than an accident.

5. **Close on a conversation (5 minutes).** Each person names one specific person they will talk to this week, and the question they will ask them. Not a pilot, not a test, not an action item. One conversation.

**Facilitator Tips:**
- Round one carries the whole session. Do not let people substitute "people would definitely want this" for an actual instance that already happened
- The cost-before-benefit finding in round two is the most common reason good community ideas die, and it is fixable once named
- Hold the close to a conversation. Groups reach for a launch plan, and a launch plan at this stage is how you find out expensively what one conversation would have told you free

**When to use it:**
- Early, before deeper planning
- When a small group needs to align fast on whether to proceed
- When time, budget, or stakeholder bandwidth is short
- As pre-work before `assets/community-audit-workshop.md`

**Going deeper:** once it is running, `assets/community-audit-workshop.md` audits an existing community from its own evidence. For a longer external framework instead, the Community Canvas (community-canvas.org, CC BY-NC-SA 4.0) maps a community across a set of named themes; see `community-frameworks.md`.

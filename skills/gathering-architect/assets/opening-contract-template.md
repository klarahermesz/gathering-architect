# The Opening Contract

A one-page card for opening a session so that everyone in the room knows why they are there, what their input actually does, and when they get their time back.

Most meeting-design tools ask you to describe the meeting. This one asks you to make three promises out loud, take one line back from every person in the room, and then check at the end whether you kept your side. The checking is what makes people believe you the next time.

---

## How to use it

**Time:** three minutes at the open, three at the close
**Who:** whoever is running the session
**When:** any session over thirty minutes where the purpose is not already obvious to everyone walking in

Fill in the first three lines before the session. Line four gets filled in by the room, live. Keep the card visible the whole time.

**Session:** _______________________________________________

**Date:** _______________________________________________

---

## 1. What we are deciding, or not deciding

Say it plainly, and say it first. The negative version is completely legitimate and often the more useful one.

Good: "By the end of this hour we will have picked one of the three options on the wall."
Also good: "We are not deciding anything today. We are gathering input for a decision Priya makes on Friday."
Not good: "To align on next steps around the roadmap."

> _We are:_ _______________________________________________

---

## 2. What your input does

This is the sentence most agendas leave out, and it is the one that decides whether people bother contributing properly. Name who decides and how much weight the room carries.

If the decision is already made, say so, and explain why you still want them there. People forgive a made decision. They do not forgive being consulted for show, and they can always tell.

Good: "I decide, but I have not made my mind up, and anything raised here changes it."
Also good: "This is already decided. I want your read on what will break when we do it."

> _Your input:_ _______________________________________________

---

## 3. When you get your time back

An end time, plus what happens if you run over. The second half matters more than the first.

Good: "We finish at 3:30. If we are not done, we stop anyway and I send round what is left."

> _We finish at:_ _______________________________________________

> _If we run over:_ _______________________________________________

---

## 4. The room's lines

Go round. Each person says, in under ten seconds, what would need to have happened for this to have been worth their time. Write them here where everyone can see them.

Keep people to ten seconds or a room of twenty will eat your entire opening. Cut them off warmly and nobody minds.

## 5. The close: read them back

Before anyone leaves, read the lines from section 4 and ask which ones got met. Mark them.

The ones that did not get met are your agenda for next time, and saying that out loud is what makes people believe section 4 was real rather than a warm-up exercise.

> _Met:_ _______________________________________________

> _Not met, so carried forward:_ _______________________________________________

---

## Facilitator notes

- **Section 2 is the one people skip**, because it is uncomfortable to say out loud. It is also the entire value of this tool. Practise the awkward version before you walk in.
- **Do not skip section 5 because you are running late.** Skipping it teaches the room that section 4 was theatre, and next time they will give you nothing.
- **For recurring meetings**, keep the same card standing and only redo sections 1 and 4. The other two rarely change.
- **For large rooms**, take section 4 from a sample of six people rather than everyone.

# The Evidence Audit

Half a day with a community that already exists, reading what its own traces say rather than asking people what they think.

Most community audits ask the organizers to describe the community. That produces the organizers' theory of the community, which is usually optimistic and always a year out of date. This one starts from artifacts. Every running community leaves traces: a message log, a queue of people who joined, a calendar, and a record of who did the work. Those four things will tell you where the problem is faster than any conversation, and they are much harder to argue with.

Offer this once someone has something running. If they are still at the idea stage, `assets/community-quick-read.md` is the earlier tool.

---

## How to run it

**Time:** 3 to 3.5 hours
**Who:** 4 to 8 people, including at least one person who joined in the last three months and at least one who mostly reads without posting
**Materials:** A wall or shared doc, and the four data pulls below **gathered in advance**

### One week out: pull the evidence

This is the part people skip, and skipping it turns the session back into an opinion survey. Someone spends about ninety minutes before the workshop collecting four things:

1. **The last 30 days of posts or messages.** You need distinct people, not message counts.
2. **The ten most recent joiners**, with the date each joined.
3. **The calendar for the last quarter**, everything that was actually scheduled.
4. **A list of tasks done in the last month**, with a name against each one.

Bring raw numbers into the room. Do not summarize them beforehand, and do not let the person who pulled them tell the room what they mean.

---

## Pull 1: Who is actually talking (35 minutes)

Take the last thirty days and count three numbers:

- How many **distinct people** posted at all?
- How many of those posted **more than twice**?
- How many posted for the **first time ever**?

Put them on the wall as a fraction of total membership. Almost every group is shocked by the first number, because message volume feels like participation and it is not. Twelve hundred messages from nine people is not a community of four hundred.

The third number is the one that predicts the future. A group where nobody posted for the first time this month is closed, whatever its join settings say.

> _Distinct posters:_ ______ / ______ members

> _Posted more than twice:_ ______

> _First-time posters:_ ______

**Watch for:** the room explaining the numbers away before they have sat with them. Give it a full minute of silence.

---

## Pull 2: What happened to the last ten joiners (45 minutes)

Not a persona. Ten real people, in a list, with their join dates. For each one, answer from the record:

- Did they post or speak within two weeks?
- Did anyone address them by name in their first two weeks?
- Are they still around?

Then, for the ones who went quiet, ask the room the only question that matters here: **what was the first thing that was asked of them?** Very often the answer is "nothing," and nothing is the problem. People who are asked for something small early stay; people who are welcomed and then left alone drift.

| Joiner | Posted in 2 wks? | Named by anyone? | Still here? | First thing asked of them |
|---|---|---|---|---|
|  |  |  |  |  |
|  |  |  |  |  |
|  |  |  |  |  |

**Watch for:** the temptation to fix this on the spot with a welcome message template. Note it and move on, you have two more pulls to do.

---

## Pull 3: What is on the calendar, and whose name is on it (40 minutes)

List the last quarter's scheduled things. For each one, write who ran it.

Then count how many distinct names appear. If it is one or two, you have found a bottleneck that is probably also a burnout risk. If a majority of entries were cancelled or quietly did not happen, that is a different and more urgent finding.

Then ask one question about the pattern rather than the entries: **which of these would still happen if nobody organized it?** The things that would happen anyway are the community's actual metabolism. The things that would stop are programming, and programming is expensive. A healthy group needs some of both, and most struggling groups are all programming.

> _Distinct organizers on the calendar:_ ______

> _Would still happen unorganized:_ _______________________________________________

> _Would stop immediately:_ _______________________________________________

---

## Pull 4: Who did the work (35 minutes)

The list of tasks from last month, with names. Count two things:

- **How many distinct names appear at all?**
- **How many tasks have exactly one name that could possibly do them?**

That second number is the concentration figure, and it is the one nobody tracks. It is not a hypothetical about someone leaving. It is a count of the tasks that currently have no second person who knows how.

Read the single-name tasks aloud. Hearing the list in one go lands differently than knowing it abstractly, and the people carrying them usually have not heard it either.

> _Distinct names:_ ______

> _Tasks with only one possible owner:_ ______ / ______ total

**Watch for:** the invisible work. Approving members, answering DMs, chasing speakers and moving furniture rarely make it onto a task list, and they are where the load actually sits. Ask specifically.

---

## Synthesis: which pull tells the most uncomfortable story? (30 minutes)

Put all four on the wall together. Each person, silently, writes down which pull disturbed them most and one sentence on why. Read them out.

There is usually a clear winner, and it is usually not the one the organizers expected walking in. Work there.

Then, one decision only:

> _The pull we are working on:_ _______________________________________________

> _The one change we are making before the next month's data:_ _______________________________________________

> _Who makes the first move, and when:_ _______________________________________________

**Re-run the same four pulls in ninety days.** The whole value of an evidence audit is that it is repeatable and the numbers are comparable. An audit you run once is just a bad afternoon.

---

## Close (15 minutes)

One person writes it up within 48 hours: the four sets of numbers, the pull chosen, the change, and the date of the next pull. Circulate it to everyone, including people who were not in the room. The numbers are the community's, not the organizers'.

---

## A worked example

A network of secondary-school science teachers across one county. Around two hundred people on the mailing list, run by two volunteer teachers who took it on when a funded programme ended.

**In the room:** the two volunteers, a teacher who joined in September, two long-standing regulars, and a head of department who reads everything and has never replied.

**Pull 1.** Two hundred and six members. Nineteen distinct posters in thirty days. Seven posted more than twice. One first-time poster. The volunteers had been describing this as "a busy list" because their inboxes were busy, and their inboxes were busy because seven people were talking to them directly.

**Pull 2.** Of the last ten joiners, three posted within two weeks and all three had been replied to by name, by the same volunteer. The seven who went quiet had been asked for nothing at all. One had joined in March and never received a single message addressed to her.

**Pull 3.** Eleven scheduled things last quarter, ten organized by the same volunteer, one by a regular. Two were cancelled. Asked which would happen anyway, the room said: the informal exam-marking swap in April, and nothing else. Everything on the calendar was programming.

**Pull 4.** Six distinct names on last month's tasks. Nine of fourteen tasks had exactly one possible owner. When they added the invisible work, the head of department pointed out that neither volunteer had listed "answering the twelve emails a week from people too nervous to post publicly," which turned out to be the single largest time cost in the whole operation.

**The uncomfortable one.** Five of six wrote Pull 2. Not the concentration, not the calendar, but the seven people asked for nothing. The volunteers had assumed the problem was that they were not running enough events, and had been about to add a monthly webinar.

**The change.** They stopped adding events. Instead, every new joiner gets asked, in their first week, for one specific small thing: a practical that works, a resource that failed, a question they are stuck on. One volunteer owns it, ten minutes a week.

**Ninety days later.** Distinct posters went from nineteen to forty-one. First-time posters from one to fourteen. The webinar they did not run would have cost roughly forty hours.
